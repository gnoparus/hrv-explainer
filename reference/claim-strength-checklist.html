Claim-Strength Checklist

Quick reference · does this citation actually say that?

Before repeating a physiological claim to a skeptical clinician, run its citation through these five checks. A citation being real isn't the same as it supporting the exact sentence it's attached to — that gap is what lesson 3 walks through in detail.

  1. Aim or side-finding? Was this the paper's stated research question, or something it turned up along the way?
  2. Cross-sectional or longitudinal? Different people compared once, or the same people tracked over time? A trend claim needs longitudinal evidence — a cross-sectional finding can't carry it.
  3. Same variable, or a stand-in? Does the paper measure the exact metric you're citing it for, or something correlated-but-different?
  4. What population? A finding validated in one domain (e.g. oncology patients) doesn't automatically generalize to another (e.g. healthy adults, or your app's demo).
  5. What's the effect size? "Statistically significant" and "explains most of the variance" are different claims — check R²/r, not just the p-value.
Worked example — lesson 3 hrvployyy's RMSSD tile cites three real papers for its biological-age framing. Running them through this checklist: Choi (2020) passes 1–2 cleanly (aim, cross-sectional group trend) but is honest cross- sectional evidence, not a trend claim. Martinez (2024) validates RMSSD (check 3) but is scoped to oncology (check 4) and says nothing about aging. Russoniello (2013) supports "HRV predicts age" as an aim, but its strongest correlate (check 3) was a different variable — maximum HR variation, not RMSSD. None of the three, alone or combined, clears check 2 for a within-person trend claim.
