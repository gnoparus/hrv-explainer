Spectral HRV Pitfalls

Lesson 1 · why LF/HF, VLF, and ULF get over-interpreted

You already know the headline: LF/HF is not a valid measure of cardiac "sympatho-vagal balance." This lesson fills in the why — the actual mechanisms behind that failure — and extends the same skepticism to VLF and ULF, the two bands your simulator (hrvployyy) doesn't even attempt to demonstrate, for good reason.

1. The orthodox story (Task Force, 1996)

The 1996 Task Force standard2 split the HRV power spectrum into four bands and assigned each a physiological story:

  • HF (0.15–0.4 Hz) — parasympathetic tone, via respiratory sinus arrhythmia.
  • LF (0.04–0.15 Hz) — originally read as sympathetic tone.
  • LF/HF — the ratio, read as "sympatho-vagal balance": high when sympathetic dominates, low when parasympathetic dominates.

This is clean, quotable, and — per the evidence below — wrong for the LF/HF part.

2. Why LF/HF fails

Billman (2013)1 lays out four reasons the LF/HF-as-balance hypothesis doesn't hold:

  • LF is not purely sympathetic. Direct sympathetic nerve recordings don't correlate with LF power. Parasympathetic activity accounts for roughly half of LF variability; sympathetic activity for roughly a quarter — the rest is unidentified.
  • The relationship is non-linear, not reciprocal. The whole ratio assumes sympathetic and parasympathetic tone move in opposite directions. They don't always. Post-exercise, sympathetic activation persists while parasympathetic tone reactivates. Cold-water immersion raises sympathetic activity but produces bradycardia via parasympathetic dominance.
  • Pharmacological blockade contradicts it directly. Randall et al. (1991) combined parasympathetic denervation with beta-blockade — removing both arms of autonomic control — and LF/HF rose from 1.1 to 8.4, falsely implying a shift toward sympathetic dominance in an animal with no functioning sympathetic drive to speak of.
  • Respiration confounds both bands independently of the ANS. Breathing rate and tidal volume shift HRV power on their own. Billman found only ~51% of studies controlled respiratory rate, and ~89% ignored tidal volume — meaning most published LF/HF numbers are partly a respiration artifact, not purely autonomic.

3. VLF and ULF: don't reach for these either

The same skepticism extends further down the spectrum, but for two different reasons that shouldn't be collapsed into one answer: VLF's problem is interpretation, ULF's is that the recording can't reach it at all.

  • VLF (0.0033–0.04 Hz) is linked to thermoregulation, the renin-angiotensin system, and intrinsic cardiac nervous system activity — but per Shaffer & Ginsberg (2017)3, "there is uncertainty regarding the physiological mechanisms responsible for activity within this band," even with an adequate recording. This app's clinical window is exactly 5 minutes (CLINICAL_WINDOW_SECONDS, src/sim/useHrvSimulation.ts) — right at Shaffer & Ginsberg's stated minimum, so VLF power can be computed here. It just shouldn't be over-interpreted once it is.
  • ULF (≤0.0033 Hz) reflects circadian rhythm and requires a full 24-hour recording to mean anything. A 5-minute recording — what this app actually simulates — cannot resolve ULF at all; there isn't a full cycle in the window, full stop.

Practical takeaway: if a committee member asks about ULF, the honest answer is "not resolvable at this recording length" — there's no number to give. If they ask about VLF, there is a number (this app's window clears the 5-minute bar), but the honest answer is that its physiological meaning is still genuinely uncertain even so — don't present it as an autonomic finding. Different question, different answer; don't reach for the same one-liner for both.
