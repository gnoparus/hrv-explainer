Does RMSSD Track Biological Age?

Lesson 3 · reading citations the way a skeptical clinician will

Lessons 1 and 2 covered claims your simulator actually implements — LF/HF banding (src/sim/psd.ts) and RSA resonance (hfAmplitudeMs()). This lesson covers a claim the app makes that nothing in the simulator models: the RMSSD tile's biological-age framing. There's no age parameter anywhere in rrGenerator.ts — the claim rests entirely on three cited papers. For your audience, that's the highest-stakes kind of claim to have on screen unrehearsed.

1. Two sentences, two different strengths

The app makes this claim in two places. Read them side by side:

RMSSD tile tooltip (MetricsStrip.tsx) — hedged:

"...HRV declines with chronological age (Choi et al. 2020), and paced-breathing HRV has been proposed as a marker of biological, not just chronological, age (Russoniello et al. 2013) — a trend worth watching across sessions, not a fixed trajectory."

Session-history panel note — asserted, not hedged "...in real repeated measurements, a rising trend tracks a younger biological-age profile and falling tracks age-typical autonomic decline (Russoniello et al. 2013; Choi et al. 2020)."

Same two citations, different confidence. One says "proposed as... a trend worth watching." The other says a rising line tracks getting younger. Only one of those is something the cited papers actually test — the evidence ladder below shows which.

2. What the three papers actually say

All three citations are real papers — that was worth checking first. The question that matters more is narrower: does each paper support the exact clause it's attached to?

  • Russoniello et al. (2013)1 — validated a wearable PPG sensor against ECG for HRV, and separately asked which HRV variable best predicts age. The variable that won, with the strongest correlation (r = −0.67), was maximum variation of heart rate — not RMSSD.
  • Choi et al. (2020)2 — cross-sectional study, 291 healthy adults ages 19–69, measured once each. Every HRV index declined with age (p<0.001); for a composite HRV-index, R² = 0.298 — real, but roughly 70% of the variance is something other than age.
  • Martinez et al. (2024)3 — a measurement-methodology review for cardiac vagal tone in oncology patients. Validates RMSSD as a good vagal-tone estimate. Says nothing about aging at all.

3. The evidence ladder

Three separate claims get bundled under "biological age." Expand each rung to see the source next to what it's actually being asked to support.

Established HRV declines with chronological age.

Choi et al. (2020), cross-sectional, n=291, ages 19–69. Every HRV index declined with age at p<0.001.

HRV-index vs. age: R² = 0.298. SDNN: R² = 0.343. LF: R² = 0.414.

This is solid, ordinary epidemiology — a real, replicated population trend. Defend it as exactly that: HRV is lower, on average, in older people. Not more.

Solid, but domain-scoped RMSSD is a validated vagal-tone outcome measure.

Martinez et al. (2024): "rMSSD is known as a good estimate of PSNS [parasympathetic] activity and reflects the short-term variation of HR."

Scope: written for cardiac vagal tone assessment in cancer patients — not a general-population aging paper. It also notes rMSSD is "not a survival prognostic factor" in that population; SDNN does better there.

Fine to cite for "RMSSD measures vagal tone" — just don't stretch it to cover aging, which this paper never discusses.

Extrapolated beyond what's shown A rising RMSSD trend across sessions means you got biologically younger.

Neither cited paper tests a within-person trend over repeated sessions.

Choi is between-person: different people measured once, not the same person tracked over time. Russoniello's strongest age correlate (r=−0.67) was maximum HR variation, not RMSSD — the metric on the app's tile isn't the one the paper validated.

This is the sentence in SessionHistory.tsx. If a clinician asks "does a rising line mean my patient got younger?", the honest answer is no — that's not what either paper measured.

4. The pitfall, named

The gap between rungs A and C is a specific, reusable mistake: a cross-sectional finding (different people, compared once) gets read as if it licensed a longitudinal claim (the same person, changing over time). A population regression line telling you older people average lower RMSSD says nothing about whether one person's RMSSD rising this week means their biological age fell. Different evidence, different claim — watch for this every time a paper is cited for a trend.

Primary source

Read before your defense — Choi et al. (2020) is the most methodologically solid of the three and the one most likely to survive a clinician's follow-up question: "Declining Trends of Heart Rate Variability According to Aging in Healthy Asian Adults".
