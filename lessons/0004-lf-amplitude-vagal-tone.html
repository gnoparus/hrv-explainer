Why Does One Slider Drive Both LF and HF?

Lesson 4 · defending a simulator shortcut instead of finding one

Lessons 1–3 checked claims about reading the simulator's output — LF/HF ratios, RSA resonance, RMSSD trends. This lesson checks a claim baked into how the simulator generates its input: vagalTone, the single 0–1 slider, scales both hfAmplitudeMs() and lfAmplitudeMs(). HF's coupling to vagal tone has a full physiological derivation in the code comments (lesson 2). LF's doesn't — it's one unexplained line:

What your simulator actually computes src/sim/rrGenerator.ts: lfAmplitudeMs(vagalTone) = A_LF_BASE_MS * (0.5 + 0.5 * vagalTone) — LF amplitude ranges 12.5–25 ms across the vagal-tone slider, with no independent "sympathetic" input anywhere in the model. If a clinician asks "why does raising vagal tone also raise your LF power?", the honest answer has to start with what the literature actually supports LF meaning — which turns out to be less settled than lesson 1's "LF is mixed origin" already suggested.

1. What's already established (lesson 1)

Billman (2013)1 found LF variability is roughly half parasympathetic, a quarter sympathetic, and a quarter unidentified. That alone means "LF tracks vagal tone" is at best a partial truth. But it gets less settled from there, not more.

2. The baroreflex reframe — and its own rebuttal

If LF isn't cleanly sympathetic, one influential proposal is that LF power instead reflects baroreflex function — the gain of the blood-pressure-to-heart-rate feedback loop — rather than any single autonomic branch's tone.2 That reframe is attractive: baroreflex gain is itself modulated by vagal tone, so it would at least gesture toward defending the app's choice.

It didn't hold up. Martelli et al. (2014)3 tested it directly — not by inference from HRV, but by recording cardiac sympathetic nerve activity straight off the nerve in sheep (pacing-induced heart failure and healthy controls) and cross-correlating it against LF HRV and directly-measured baroreflex sensitivity. Their conclusion: LF HRV is "neither a robust index of CSNA nor of BRS" — not sympathetic tone, and not baroreflex sensitivity either, contradicting the very reframe that was supposed to rescue LF's interpretability.

3. The evidence ladder

Three claims, each weaker than the last:

Established LF variability has mixed autonomic origin — not purely sympathetic.

Billman (2013): parasympathetic activity accounts for roughly half of LF variability, sympathetic for roughly a quarter, the rest unidentified.

This is the one solid floor under everything else in this lesson.

Proposed, then directly refuted LF power reflects baroreflex gain instead of any single autonomic branch.

Rahman & Goldstein (2011): "LF power seems to provide an index not of cardiac sympathetic tone but of baroreflex function."

Martelli et al. (2014), using implanted-electrode cardiac sympathetic nerve recordings in sheep: "LF HRV is neither a robust index of CSNA nor of BRS."

The 2011 reframe was reasonable when it was made. The 2014 study is the stronger evidence — a direct nerve recording beats an inference from HRV alone — and it goes against the reframe. Cite Goldstein only alongside Martelli, not instead of it.

Not supported by any cited source Scaling LF amplitude off the same "vagal tone" slider that drives HF is a physiologically validated choice.

No paper here validates LF amplitude as a clean vagal-tone marker either — that claim was never on the table. Every mechanism tested (sympathetic tone, baroreflex sensitivity) has been tested and rejected by direct measurement. This isn't a defended choice; it's an undefended simplification.

4. The honest answer, not the defensive one

The instinct is to search for a citation that makes the shared-slider design "correct." There isn't one — and reaching for Goldstein (2011) alone would hand a well-read clinician an easy rebuttal via Martelli (2014). The defensible answer is different: no single scalar cleanly explains LF amplitude, so the simulator doesn't pretend one does. One slider driving both bands is a stated simplification for a single interactive demo, not a claim that LF and HF share a physiological driver. That's a weaker-sounding answer than "we modeled the mechanism" — and it's the true one.
